I Thought I Was Being Asked Whether Discrimination Should Be Prohibited. I Wasn't.

Wisconsin's Question 3 sounds simple. What a Yes or No vote actually does is considerably less obvious.

By Windy Lynn Warren
September 22, 2026

Author's note: I used AI to help with editing and fact-checking this article. The personal experiences and opinions are mine.

When I first read Question 3 on Wisconsin's upcoming November ballot, I thought I understood it.

The heading says, “Governmental entity discrimination prohibited.”

My first reaction was simple: Of course discrimination should be prohibited. Who would vote in favor of it?

But that is not really the choice we are being asked to make.

I had to read the question several times. Then I had to research what a yes vote would do and what a no vote would leave alone.

If I could misunderstand it, I imagine other voters could, too.

I am not writing this to tell anyone how to vote. I want to explain the question in ordinary language so people can decide for themselves — and make sure the answer they mark matches what they actually believe.

Here is Question 3 exactly as it will appear on the ballot:

“Shall section 27 of article I of the constitution be created to prohibit governmental entities in the state from discriminating against, or granting preferential treatment to, any individual or group on the basis of race, sex, color, ethnicity, or national origin in public employment, public education, public contracting, or public administration?”

That is a lot to take in while standing in a voting booth.

What Do YES and NO Mean?

A yes vote would add a new rule to the Wisconsin Constitution.

That rule could stop or limit public programs that give someone added consideration because of race, sex, color, ethnicity, or national origin.

A no vote would not add the new rule. It would leave the current system in place, with programs continuing to be judged under the state and federal laws we already have.

Voting no would not make discrimination legal. Discrimination is already against the law, and those protections remain whether Question 3 passes or fails.

In the simplest terms:

YES adds the new rule and could limit these programs.

NO does not add the rule and leaves the current system in place.

A no vote does not guarantee that every current program will continue. Programs can still be reviewed, challenged, or changed under laws that already exist. It simply means Wisconsin would not add this particular rule to its Constitution.

What Kinds of Programs Could Be Affected?

Question 3 covers more than government jobs.

It applies to public employment, public education, government contracts, and other government-run programs. It reaches state and local government, public school districts, Wisconsin's technical colleges, the University of Wisconsin System, the Legislature, and the courts.

Consider public education.

Question 3 would not prevent women or students of color from attending Madison College or a UW school. However, because the amendment covers public education, it could affect programs that give someone an opportunity — or added consideration — because of race, sex, color, ethnicity, or national origin.

Some limits already exist. In June 2026, for example, the Wisconsin Supreme Court ruled against a state higher-education grant program that used race-conscious eligibility criteria, and the Universities of Wisconsin stopped making new awards under those criteria. That matters because some of these programs are already being challenged or changed under laws we have now.

Question 3 also applies outside the classroom.

Wisconsin already has programs meant to help businesses owned by women, minorities, and disabled veterans compete for state business. Some of those programs set goals for how much business the state does with these companies. Wisconsin also has a 5% bidding preference for certified minority-owned businesses, although that preference is currently paused.

A public contract could involve building a school, supplying equipment, completing roadwork, or providing a service for a city.

And because Question 3 specifically includes public contracts, those kinds of programs are part of this conversation, too.

A no vote would leave those programs and policies to be judged under the laws already in place.

These are the kinds of real-life effects that are difficult to see when the heading only says, “Governmental entity discrimination prohibited.”

I Was Given an Opportunity

This is not just a political argument to me.

In 1988, affirmative action helped give me an opportunity to attend college. I was a B student, and I remain grateful for that chance.

It did not complete my assignments, take my tests, or earn my degree for me. It helped open a door. I still had to walk through it and do the work.

Would a program exactly like the one that helped me exist today? I cannot say. Laws and college practices have changed since 1988. But my experience helps me understand the difference between receiving an opportunity and being handed an accomplishment.

Those are not the same thing.

The Same Thing — or a Fair Chance?

Supporters of Question 3 believe the government should treat everyone exactly the same. They believe the five traits named in the question should not be used to favor or disadvantage anybody.

Opponents believe treating everybody exactly the same does not always give everyone a fair chance, especially when some people have faced barriers that others have not.

A wheelchair ramp is not an unfair advantage over the person who takes the stairs. It is what allows both people through the same door.

Disability is not one of the five traits named in Question 3. I use the ramp as an everyday example of the larger question: Is fairness always about giving people the exact same thing, or can it sometimes mean removing a barrier so people have a more equal chance?

Reasonable people may answer that differently.

That is one of the questions buried inside Question 3.

Why the Wording Matters

A person may read “discrimination prohibited,” oppose discrimination, and vote yes without realizing that the new rule could also affect programs meant to open opportunities.

Another person may vote no because they believe some of those programs should remain possible. That does not mean they support discrimination.

This is what troubled me about the wording. It can create an immediate emotional response before the voter understands what either answer would do.

Changing the Wisconsin Constitution is also different from passing an ordinary law. The Constitution contains the basic rules our state government must follow. If Wisconsin later wanted to remove or rewrite this rule, it would take another lengthy process and another statewide vote.

That deserves more than a quick reaction to a heading.

Vote according to what you believe. But before you fill in yes or no, ask what you believe the government should be allowed to do.

Then make sure the answer you mark is the answer you mean.
